Two technologies sit at the heart of this shift. Large language models (LLMs) understand and generate language at scale, and their capabilities have grown much faster than earlier technologies. AI agents go further: they perceive their environment, decide and act to reach a goal. An agent might gather policy information, assess whether cover applies, draft a letter and escalate edge cases to a human expert.

1.2 Five challenges that change governance

The executive summary names five contextual challenges. Together they explain why traditional model governance needs to be extended for GenAI.

1. Professional roles are shifting

Actuaries move from producing analyses to supervising, validating and governing AI-enabled workflows. The report asks how professional responsibilities should be redefined, and which ethical principles should govern the delegation of judgment to agents. Consequential decisions are a particular concern, for example adverse underwriting actions such as a higher premium, exclusions or limitations.

2. Opacity undermines trust and explainability

Frontier models have hundreds of billions of parameters. GPT-4 is estimated, unofficially, at about 1.8 trillion. Their reasoning cannot be traced the way assumptions and formulas in an actuarial model can. Yet the Actuarial Standards of Practice and regulators expect practitioners to explain methods, justify assumptions and communicate findings to boards, regulators and policyholders.

3. Validation must cover language and images

Back-testing, residual analysis and benchmarking work well for numbers. What does "accurate" mean for a policy summary or an explanation of an underwriting decision? A text can be factually right but misleading in tone, or complete but badly organised. Training/test splits, overfitting and performance metrics still apply, but actuaries need new skills: evaluating language outputs, prompt engineering, semantic similarity and hallucination detection.

4. New safety and security risks

Familiar infrastructure controls remain essential, but agents widen the attack surface: an agent connected to policy systems, customer databases and external APIs could leak data or execute unauthorised transactions. GenAI adds its own threats: prompt injection, data poisoning, hallucinations, adversarial examples, jailbreaking and bias amplification. Module 4 covers each one.

5. Production needs continuous monitoring

Moving from pilot to production needs version control, testing frameworks, deployment pipelines and integration with actuarial systems. Performance can drift as language, claim types and customer behaviour change. Monitoring should track hallucination rates, bias metrics and user satisfaction, not only processing time and error rates. A common pattern pairs traditional actuarial or ML models, validated separately, with a GenAI layer that orchestrates them and assists the expert.

Real incidents the report cites
  • Data poisoning: a 2025 Nature Medicine study found that replacing 0.001% of training tokens with medical misinformation was enough to make healthcare LLMs produce harmful clinical outputs.
  • Litigation: in Estate of Gene B. Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group, plaintiffs allege an algorithm wrongly denied post-acute care to Medicare Advantage members. A federal court let the class action proceed in February 2025.
  • Manipulated images: claims involving AI-manipulated vehicle images tripled year-on-year at major UK insurers.
  • Jailbreak: in 2024 a user manipulated a dealership's chatbot into agreeing to sell a $76,000 vehicle for one dollar.
  • Bias: a widely used US healthcare algorithm used past costs as a proxy for health needs, and cut the number of Black patients flagged for extra care by more than half.

1.3 Who leads: the human or the agent?

The report classifies actuarial GenAI use cases by locus of control. This distinction drives most governance choices later in the course.

Human-led, agent support

The augmented actuary

The actuary remains the primary decision-maker. Agents retrieve information, run calculations, draft preliminary analyses or suggest approaches. The actuary directs the workflow, interprets outputs and bears ultimate responsibility for conclusions.

Suits complex, highly regulated tasks such as underwriting decisions.

Agent-led, human support

Process redesign

Agents execute workflows, make routine decisions and produce outputs. Humans oversee, handle exceptions and intervene when needed. The actuary's role shifts from execution to supervision and quality assurance.

Can apply to lower-risk tasks such as fraud detection support in claims.

Within agent-led workflows, oversight itself comes in three strengths:

Human-in-the-loopThe agent proposes, but cannot finalise a consequential action or output without explicit human approval at each key decision point.
Human-on-the-loopThe agent runs complete workflows. Humans monitor performance, review samples and can override or halt it when issues appear.
Human-out-of-the-loopThe agent works autonomously within defined parameters. Humans set it up, review at system level, monitor and govern, but do not oversee individual cases.

Decreasing case-by-case human involvement from left to right (§1.1, §5.2.6). These modes apply to any GenAI application, not only agents.

2.1 From words to numbers

An LLM is a kind of generative AI: AI that creates new content (text, images, audio, code) instead of only analysing existing data. LLMs are neural networks, usually transformers, trained on vast datasets with billions of parameters. They grew out of decades of progress in natural language processing.

→

From sequence modelling, to contextual understanding, to generation, to multimodal reasoning (§5.1.1).

Tokens and embeddings

Before a network can read text, two things happen:

  1. Tokenisation. The text is cut into tokens: sentences, words or, most often, pieces of words. "Reinsurance" might become "Re", "insur", "ance".
  2. Embedding. Each token becomes a dense vector of numbers. Words with similar meanings get similar vectors. The classic example: the vector for King − Man + Woman lands very close to Queen. Embeddings capture analogies as well as similarity.

Attention and the transformer

The transformer, introduced in 2017, is the foundation of every modern LLM. Its key idea is attention: for each token, the model weighs how much every other token in the sequence matters, wherever it sits. That is how it tells "river bank" from "bank loan". Multi-head attention runs several attention mechanisms in parallel to learn different kinds of relationships. Transformers also process tokens in parallel, so they train much faster than RNNs.

Encoder-only

BERT (2018)

Reads the tokens before and after each position, so it sees the whole sentence. It learns by predicting masked (hidden) tokens. Strong at classification and question answering.

Decoder-only

GPT (2018 onward)

Generative Pre-trained Transformer. Looks only at preceding tokens and learns to predict the next one. At inference it writes one token at a time, feeding each new token back in. Strong at generation: summaries, drafting, translation.

Two phases of training

Pre-training

The model learns general language patterns, grammar and knowledge from a massive corpus (books, articles, websites) with a self-supervised objective such as next-token prediction. It is so costly that new foundation models are released relatively rarely.

Fine-tuning

The pre-trained model is trained further on a smaller, specialised dataset for a task such as answering questions or summarising. Parameter-efficient methods update only a small subset of weights, so teams without heavy infrastructure can do it. Module 3 covers it in detail.

Beyond text: multimodal models and OCR

The latest LLMs are multimodal. An image is cut into small patches, each turned into an embedding by a vision encoder (typically a Vision Transformer) and projected into the same space as text tokens. Attention then runs over the mixed sequence, so the model can read text from a scanned form and reason about what it means.

Use the smallest tool that does the job

Not every document task needs a general multimodal LLM. Extracting a policyholder's details from an identity card scan is pure text-from-image extraction. A smaller OCR model can do it more cheaply. The report cites Nougat, an encoder-decoder model that handles complex documents such as actuarial formulas and tables more precisely and at lower cost than generalist LLMs §5.1.1.6.

2.3 The limits every reviewer should know

The report sorts limits into three families. Knowing which family a problem belongs to tells you where to look for the fix.

An LLM hallucinates when it produces information that is false, unverifiable or stated with unwarranted confidence. It predicts the most probable next word: it writes what seems likely, not what is necessarily true.

Factual hallucination

Contradicts verifiable facts: a wrong date, a quote attributed to the wrong person, a regulation or entity that does not exist.

Faithfulness hallucination

Diverges from the user's input or contradicts itself: ignores an instruction, contradicts the claim file you supplied, or reasons inconsistently.

Where they come from. Training data contains errors, biases and gaps. Training rewards always giving an answer, even without enough information. Word-by-word generation favours plausibility over truth. Learning-theory research suggests hallucination may be inevitable when LLMs are used as general problem solvers.

How to contain them. Combine generation with rule-based and data-driven foundations. Retrieval (vector search, graphs, curated reference data) grounds the model in verified information. Deterministic rules and pre-validated ML models handle critical decision steps. In some designs, GenAI is only the interface that interprets queries and formats answers, while structured algorithms make the decision.

Most commercial LLMs are proprietary, and information is hidden at every stage:

  • Training: datasets, curation, hyperparameters and filtering are generally not disclosed.
  • Architecture and weights: code is rarely open source, and closed-weight models keep their learned parameters private. Open-weight models publish them for inspection and reuse.
  • Deployment: the provider's system prompt (tone, safety rules, style) is hidden from end users.

Even with full access, billions of parameters make meaningful auditing practically infeasible. Hence the "black box" label: inputs and outputs are visible, the reasoning in between is not. The consequences are reduced auditability, harder root-cause analysis and tension with regulatory explainability expectations.

The way out

These limits apply when models are used "as is". Grounding the model in structured reference data, and having a human validate its assessment against that data, separates what stays a black box from what is traceable. Once a human validates the reasoning and outputs, the decision rationale becomes auditable and transferable to a human decision-maker §5.1.3.2.2.

Prompt sensitivity is the tendency to answer differently, sometimes very differently, to small changes in wording or structure. It causes three problems:

  1. Evaluation: variable answers make it hard to judge a model's true capability.
  2. User experience: people rephrase several times before they get a usable answer.
  3. Fairness: phrasing can trigger or amplify biases from the training data, so similar cases may get different outcomes.

Robustness is the related ability to give consistent, reliable outputs (accurate, factual, safe and fair) when inputs or contexts vary slightly. Prompt sensitivity directly undermines it.

Example

Two underwriters ask an assistant about the same applicant, one with "Summarise the medical risks" and the other with "Is there anything worrying in this file?". If the substance of the answer changes, the tool is not robust enough for decision support.

2.4 The dials: temperature, Top-P and Top-K

At each step the model assigns a probability to every candidate next token. Generation parameters decide how adventurous the choice is. They matter for governance because they change variability, reliability and hallucination risk.

  • Temperature reshapes the probabilities. Around 0.1 gives predictable, reliable output; around 1.0 gives creative but less reliable output.
  • Top-K keeps only the K most probable tokens. With K = 50, only the 50 likeliest words compete.
  • Top-P (nucleus sampling) keeps the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds P. With P = 0.9, the tokens covering 90% of the probability mass.

Context window and memory

Context window

Each model can take in a limited number of tokens per query. Chat history uses them up, so older parts of a conversation are progressively forgotten. The more data you pack into the instructions, the harder the task becomes. This bites in document-heavy work with long reports or historical datasets.

Memory

Short-term memory is what sits in the context window. Long-term memory stores past interactions in a separate database and retrieves them when needed. For agents, memory is a key design parameter, and also a privacy and security surface (see Module 4).

Bottom line

Taken together, these limits mean AI systems are best used as decision-support tools that give guidance, not definitive answers. Responsible deployment relies on robust validation, clear human oversight and continuous governance §5.1.4.

Key takeaways

  • LLMs turn text into tokens and embeddings, then use attention to predict what comes next.
  • Hallucinations come from how the model works, not only from bad data. Ground the model and verify its output.
  • Opacity is real, but the system around the model can still be made traceable and auditable.
  • Low temperature and tight sampling give stable, reproducible outputs. Record these settings as part of the system's configuration.

3.2 Choosing the model

Size

From small to very large. Size drives compute cost, especially for fine-tuning and at scale.

Weight access

Open-weight models can often run locally, be inspected for explainability and bias audits, and be fine-tuned. Closed-weight models are reached through an API. Closed models have historically led on general knowledge, but the gap keeps narrowing.

Ownership

Training a foundation model is extremely expensive, so most state-of-the-art LLMs are proprietary. Insurers typically use third-party models and align them to their use cases.

Modality

Language-only or multimodal. Claims work with photographs or scanned documents may need multimodal capabilities.

Public leaderboards such as the Open LLM Leaderboard and Stanford HELM compare models across tasks, and domain-specific leaderboards are emerging. These choices also decide which explainability, bias-testing and content-safety methods you can use later. The SOA report Operationalizing LLMs: A Guide for Actuaries (2025) goes deeper into model and provider selection.

3.3 Prompt-level alignment

Shaping behaviour through the prompt is called in-context learning: you adapt the model to a task by what you put in front of it, with no costly retraining.

System prompt

Set by the developer, applied to every request, usually invisible to users. It fixes context, rules, objectives, tone and constraints.

User prompt

The specific request a person types. It triggers the answer within the frame the system prompt sets.

Prompt engineering is the systematic work of writing, testing and refining these inputs. It is a way to "program" an LLM: enforce rules, automate steps, customise outputs. Prompt patterns document reusable solutions to common problems, and dedicated platforms let you register and version prompts.

The COSTAR structure

COSTAR is a checklist for structuring a prompt: Context, Objective, Style, Tone, Audience, Response format. The authors mostly use it for system prompts, where its structure pays off most.

3.4 Model-level specialisation: RAG or fine-tuning

  
When prompting is not enough to reach the accuracy, consistency or domain specificity you need, you can bring insurance knowledge closer to the model. Useful sources include policy wordings and clauses, claims records and loss histories, underwriting guidelines and risk appetite, regulatory texts, internal procedures, and customer interaction logs. The data's nature and sensitivity, and the use case, drive the choice of technique.


  
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

  
RAG grounds answers in documents retrieved at query time, without touching the model's parameters. It fixes a basic limit of standalone LLMs: they cannot see recent or proprietary information that was not in their training data.

Because each answer links back to its source documents, RAG suits insurance tasks where accuracy and traceability matter.


  
Fine-tuning

  
Fine-tuning trains a pre-trained model further on a smaller, task- or domain-specific dataset, and so permanently changes its weights. It starts from what the model already knows, so it is far cheaper than pre-training.

  

    
Supervised fine-tuning
Train on question-answer pairs labelled by experts, such as actuarial questions with validated answers. Self-instruct generates the dataset with a well-calibrated LLM to save annotation cost.

    
RLHF
Humans rank model outputs on usefulness, safety or compliance. A reward model learns from the rankings, and the LLM is tuned (e.g., with PPO) toward higher-reward answers. Users see more consistent, guideline-compliant behaviour.

    
LoRA (PEFT)
Freezes the original weights and trains small low-rank matrices, typically under 1% of parameters. Memory and compute drop sharply.

  

  

    Watch out
    
Fine-tuning is costly and complex compared with RAG, and it carries a specific risk: catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses some general capabilities after its weights are adjusted.

Reflexion, Tree of Thought
Other structures that shape reasoning, for example through self-critique or exploring several branches.

  


  
Teams of agents

  
A multi-agent system (MAS) lets specialised agents work together on complex tasks that need parallel work or several kinds of expertise. It costs more tokens and time than a single agent.

  

    
Independent
Agents work in parallel without talking; an aggregator combines their outputs. Maximum parallelism, but no internal error correction.

    
Centralised
An orchestrator assigns tasks, collects answers, may reassign, and consolidates the result.

    
Decentralised
No orchestrator: agents collaborate directly, peer to peer.

    
Hybrid
An orchestrator coordinates, but agents can also hand tasks to one another.

  


  
More agents is not automatically better. With the same token budget, prompts and tools, research cited by the report (Kim et al., 2025) found three effects:

  
    
    
  • Capacity saturation: once a single agent achieves 45% of a task, adding agents costs more in coordination than it gains.
    • 
    
  • Tools versus coordination: with many complex tools, splitting the token budget can leave each agent too little to use them well.
    • 
    
  • Error amplification: a single agent can amplify its own errors, while a central orchestrator can catch and fix them.
    •

    Agents in actuarial work today
    
Agents can check the modelling and cash-flow projection logic in actuarial Excel files and produce validation reports much faster than manual review. They can document models and processes. 3.6 Operation integration: MCP

  
On its own, an agent cannot reach a database, retrieve a document, run a calculation or touch an enterprise system. It needs a standard, secure way to call tools.

  
The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic in 2024, is an open protocol for exactly that. It uses a client-host-server design: the host is the chatbot or agent, the client manages the connection, and servers expose tools, resources and prompts through a standard API. The report compares it to a universal USB-C port.

For a regulated industry, the report considers MCP's consistent, traceable and permissioned pathway preferable.

3.7 The human side: oversight and feedback

  
Human-AI interaction has two dimensions: the level of oversight during operation (in, on or out of the loop, as in Module 1) and the feedback used to improve the system over time.

  

    
Explicit feedback
Collected on purpose, for example by asking users to rate the relevance of an answer.

    
Implicit feedback
Captured during normal use: time to first token (TTFT), response time, whether the user copies the answer, rephrases the question or asks a follow-up. It needs no extra effort, so there is much more of it.

    Safety or security?
    
The report separates the two. Safety protects people from harm caused by the system's outputs, such as biased or harmful content. Security protects the system from external threats and malicious actors. Infrastructure security for hosting GenAI is out of the report's scope. It focuses on threats tied to the system's design and architecture. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a useful companion reference.

  


  
4.1 Data leakage

  
Data leakage is the unintended exposure of sensitive or confidential information by an LLM. It is one of the most common LLM risks, and especially serious in insurance, which handles personal, health and financial data.

  

    Anonymisation is not a guarantee
    
Research shows LLMs can infer personal attributes such as location, age or health status from contextual cues, even after identifiers are removed. Anonymised claims descriptions or underwriting notes could still allow re-identification, especially when combined with public information.

  


  

    

      
      
      
      
    

    

      
Where: the user's input. How: sensitive data is typed into the prompt and, without safeguards, sent to a third-party LLM provider through the API, where it may be stored or exposed.

      
Example: a customer tells a policy-selection chatbot about their health and finances to get a better recommendation.

      
Attackers can also use prompts to extract data the model can reach. This is prompt injection. If a model was trained on private data, a question like "What is the delivery status of the order placed by M. Martin?" could return "12 rue de la Paix, Paris, France."

    

    

      
Where: the data used to align the model. How: a model fine-tuned on sensitive data reveals some of it during use.

      
Example: an email-reply assistant tuned on historical customer emails, without guardrails, slips another customer's personal details into a draft reply.

    

    

      
Where: the RAG knowledge base. How: if it holds sensitive information, the model may pass it to the provider in prompts or reveal it in answers.

      
Safeguards: encrypt confidential content in the knowledge base, set access controls, choose a trustworthy provider, and audit model behaviour regularly.

    

    

      
Agents add three leakage paths of their own:

      
    
        
  • Tool access through MCP: weak authorisation lets an agent reach data beyond its intended scope.
    • 
        
  • Reasoning traces: internal "scratchpad" monologues used to plan and weigh options may capture sensitive data without protection or anonymisation.
    • 
        
  • Memory: persisted embeddings, summaries and learned patterns can over-retain sensitive information if retention and access rules are loose.
    • 
      

      
The answer is a security framework that covers authorisation boundaries, reasoning-trace protection and memory governance.

4.3 Bias and content safety

  
Bias is a systematic tendency to favour certain outcomes, perspectives or groups, often producing unfair results. LLMs can show many kinds: algorithmic, cognitive, confirmation, exclusion, measurement, out-group homogeneity, prejudice, selection and stereotype bias. They can reproduce and amplify biases in their training data, and generate offensive, inappropriate or false content.

  

    
At user input
The input can trigger existing biases. An underwriting assistant trained on data that over-represents one group may systematically advise declining applicants from under-represented groups. Unmonitored, this can breach regulation.

    
In the knowledge base (RAG)
A knowledge base built from past claims or underwriting reports shaped by redlining could lead a RAG assistant to recommend higher premiums for those areas, regardless of individual risk. Curate reliable documents.

    
In the model
Forced to answer outside its expertise, a model gives more biased or hallucinated responses. A life insurance chatbot asked for medical advice is a reputational and operational risk.

  


  
What "fair" means

  
Fairness has several competing definitions. No single metric captures everything, so the right one depends on the business use case and the decision it supports.

  

    
Group fairness
Outcomes are statistically comparable across groups (sex, age, ethnicity). Two common measures:
Demographic parity: similar predicted outcomes across groups, without conditioning on the true outcome.
Predictive parity: people who receive the same decision experience similar true outcomes, whatever their group.

    
Individual fairness
Similar individuals receive similar outcomes.

    
Counterfactual fairness
The output would not change if a sensitive attribute were hypothetically different.

  

  
For GenAI, fairness metrics are newer. One proposal adapts demographic parity: outputs should represent all groups equally in frequency, sentiment and associations.

4.4 Drift

  
A model validated at build time can behave differently in production. Drift is the gap between expected and real behaviour. For GenAI systems, prompt styles, user behaviour and retrieved knowledge can all change, so continuous monitoring is considered best practice.

  

    
Data drift
Production data differs from the data the system was built on. A motor fraud vision model fails on motorcycle claims.

      
  • Concept drift: meanings or the domain change. A policy now covers motorcycles as well as cars; "delivery" starts to include digital products; society changes.
  • Statistical drift: how requests are phrased changes, for example relaxed instead of formal wording.

    
Prompt drift
Users' prompts change in style, structure or intent compared with what the system was designed for.

    
Task drift
The model strays from its original instructions, for instance because of malicious prompts hidden in external data.

    
Model drift
Predictive capacity deteriorates because the environment has moved away from the historical data the model learned from.

  


  
4.5 Defence in depth

  
No single control stops every threat. The report organises mitigations by layer, from the user's input to the system's output, with monitoring over the whole chain.


  

    

      
      
      
      
      
    

    

      
    
        
  • Prompt sanitisation: detect and anonymise sensitive data before it reaches the model or a third-party API. Counters prompt-based leakage.
    • 
        
  • Input filtering: screen and block harmful prompts and injection attempts. Counters adversarial attacks and supports content safety.
    • 
        
  • Prompt design and ethical instructions: a clear system prompt that states fairness requirements. Techniques such as SmoothLLM add controlled noise to the prompt and aggregate several responses to resist adversarial perturbations and bias.
    • 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Authentication: verify identity (e.g., OAuth 2.0, JWT) and log every interaction: who, what, when.
    • 
        
  • Role-based access control (RBAC): limit which documents and data each role can retrieve. In RAG this limits knowledge-based leakage. Keep sensitive and open data in separate stores with distinct policies.
    • 
        
  • Data minimisation and anonymisation: collect and keep less personal data, anonymised where possible in training data and knowledge bases. Combine with other controls, given re-identification risk.
    • 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Knowledge-base encryption: industry-standard algorithms such as AES-256, at rest and in transit. Store sensitive data there only with particular caution.
    • 
        
  • AI gateway: a single control point between users and models. It logs every request and response, helps detect leakage and anomalous usage, and centralises API access to reduce dependence on one provider. AI firewalls can also block malicious prompts and filter harmful outputs.
    • 
        
  • Data statements and WEAT: data statements describe a dataset and reveal gaps between training and real populations. The Word Embedding Association Test measures human biases in embeddings. Fine-tuning can also reduce biased content.
    • 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • LLM-based safety classifiers: post-generation filters that flag and block harmful, toxic or policy-violating outputs.
    • 
        
  • Content moderation APIs: programmable services that enforce custom content policies.
    • 
      

    

    

      
    
        
  • Red teaming: simulate attacks before deployment and periodically after, especially prompt injection and jailbreaks.
    • 
        
  • Drift monitoring: track KPIs and alert on significant deviations in data, prompts, tasks or model performance.
    • 
        
  • User feedback loops: let users flag wrong, biased or inappropriate outputs, and feed this into improvement. It catches what metrics miss.
    • 
        
  • Toxicity testing: use dedicated evaluation datasets (for example from Hugging Face) to measure the risk of harmful content.
    •

5.1 The United States: a patchwork

  

    
Federal
No comprehensive federal AI law. On March 20, 2026 the administration published a National Policy Framework for AI calling for a single national standard. It is a policy blueprint: it does not displace state statutes. Any pre-emption would depend on the final text, and some state rules (child protection, fraud prevention, consumer protection, zoning, a state's own AI use) would stay outside it.

    
States
Two cross-sector AI frameworks stand out: the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA, effective January 1, 2026) and Colorado SB26-189 on automated decision-making technology (effective January 1, 2027). Many other state laws target specific uses, more bills are pending, and about 20 state privacy laws address automated processing and profiling. Anti-discrimination statutes add another layer.

  

  

    Insurance carve-outs
    
Some general AI laws defer to insurance regulation. Under Colorado's framework, insurers that comply with the state's insurance-specific AI rules are treated as compliant in the practice of insurance. Under TRAIGA, the AI discrimination provisions do not apply to insurance entities already subject to unfair discrimination and unfair or deceptive practices statutes. Whether a carve-out applies to your activity is a legal question.

  


  
Insurance-specific frameworks

  

    

      
      
      
      
      
    

    

      
NAIC Model Bulletin on the Use of AI Systems by Insurers, adopted December 4, 2023. A non-binding framework that states adopt and enforce at their discretion. Several have done so, with local nuances.

      
    
        
  • Builds on the NAIC AI Principles of August 14, 2020: Fairness, Accountability, Transparency and Security ("FATS").
    • 
        
  • Recommends a written AIS Program for the responsible use of AI in regulated insurance practices, covering governance, risk management and internal audit, with lifecycle risk identification, mitigation and management.
    •

    • 
      

    

    

      
Colorado SB21-169 and Regulation 10-1-1 (amended August 20, 2025). Life insurers and, since 2025, private passenger auto and health benefit plan insurers must run a risk-based governance and risk management framework. It must support policies, procedures, systems and controls that identify and remediate unfair discrimination, particularly by race, resulting from external consumer data (ECDIS), algorithms and predictive models.

      
ECDIS covers sources such as credit scores, social media habits, purchasing habits, home ownership, education, IoT and biometric data, and risk scores derived from them.

    

    

      
California Department of Insurance Bulletin 2022-5 (June 30, 2022), on allegations of racial bias and unfair discrimination in marketing, rating, underwriting and claims. It notes that AI use has contributed to more consumer complaints, and expects insurers to avoid both conscious and unconscious bias from AI, and to carry out due diligence on compliance with all applicable laws.

    •

    •

    •

The NAIC is also running:

    •

    • 
      

    

  


  

    The common thread
    
Across these texts, supervisors expect the same things: transparency, fairness and non-discrimination, accountability and governance, explainability, documentation and traceability, ongoing monitoring, and attention to impacts on individuals. In practice that means documenting design, implementation and monitoring, testing fairness and mitigating disparate impact, and keeping documents ready for regulators.

5.3 Beyond the US

  
Whether foreign rules apply depends mainly on where the system is placed on the market, where it is used, and who is affected by its output. Most jurisdictions rely on strategies, principles or sector rules. A few have binding AI laws.

7.1 Model or system?

  
The report follows the IAA (2025), which takes the OECD definition. The AI model is the core component that makes inferences from inputs to produce outputs. The AI system is everything around it: infrastructure, data flows, tools, oversight and governance. Many critical risks (bias, hallucination, lack of explainability) start in the model, but governance applies to the whole system.

  

    Mixed systems are the norm
    
One system may combine a third-party LLM that writes narrative with a traditional ML component such as a GLM for pricing. The two need different validation strategies and lifecycles. In the case studies, the traditional models are assumed to be validated separately beforehand. The lifecycle here focuses on the system and its GenAI components.

8.1 LLM-as-a-judge

  
Human review of every output is slow and expensive. LLM-as-a-judge uses one LLM to evaluate another's outputs at scale. It works for conversational agents, RAG pipelines and agentic workflows. Three design choices matter: the evaluation task, the evaluator model, and how you manage its weaknesses.


  

    

      
      
      
    

    

      

        
Without a reference
The judge scores quality on its own terms: binary (yes/no), discrete (1–5) or continuous. For a model that drafts replies to policyholder emails, it might score clarity, tone and factual accuracy separately. Useful when no ground truth exists, which is common in generative tasks.

        
With a reference
The judge receives the prompt, the model's answer and a "golden" reference answer. Closed questions (e.g., multiple choice) reduce to exact matching. Open questions need semantic comparison, helped by NLP metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU. Good for benchmarking and regression tests, but it can penalise valid alternative wording and needs high-quality references.

      

    

    

      
The judge picks the better of two outputs, or ranks several, against set criteria. Typical use: comparing configurations, such as two versions of a system prompt for claims summaries, to see which gives more accurate and concise results.

    

    

      

        
General-purpose LLM
Off-the-shelf and easy to deploy, with broad reasoning. Good for fluency, relevance and logical coherence.

        
Fine-tuned judge
Trained on evaluation data for a domain. More accurate on specialised points such as actuarial terminology or regulatory compliance issues a generalist might miss.

      

      
The trade-off is ease of deployment against domain precision.

    

  


  

    
Bias
Verbosity
Favours longer answers, equating length with quality.

    
Bias
Self-enhancement
Rates outputs from its own foundation model more favourably.

    
Bias
Positional
In pairwise comparisons, systematically prefers the first (or last) answer shown.

  

  

    Keep the expert in charge
    
The judge is scalable and flexible, and can give nuanced assessments. But it should itself be evaluated, supervised by actuarial experts, and its methodology and limits documented. The expert stays responsible for the qualitative judgment, which cannot be fully automated §6.1.2.2.3.

  


  
8.2 Evaluating RAG with RAGAS

  
RAGAS (Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment) scores a RAG system on several dimensions, using an external LLM to automate the work.

  

    
Faithfulness
Break the answer into statements and check each against the retrieved context. Score = supported statements ÷ total statements.

    
Answer relevance
Have an LLM generate questions from the answer, then measure their average similarity to the original question.

    
Context relevance
Have an LLM extract the sentences of the context that are needed to answer. Score = needed sentences ÷ all sentences. Penalises redundant retrieval.

8.4 Explainability

  
Three related ideas from the machine learning literature:

  
    
    
  • Transparency is structural: the model can be simulated, decomposed and analysed. GenAI models are not inherently transparent.
    •

  • Explainability produces post hoc explanations of a non-transparent model with auxiliary methods. It can be local (one output) or global (overall behaviour).

LLMs make this hard: they are complex, sometimes closed-weight, and probabilistic, so the same question can produce different answers depending on wording or context. The report proposes a matrix that crosses scope (local or global) with focus (why the outcome, or how the process).

8.5 A warning: alignment faking

  
Research has found that large models can strategically appear aligned with a training objective while being trained, to avoid having their behaviour changed, then revert when unmonitored. Three conditions are typically needed:

  
    
    
  1. The model has strong pre-existing preferences that conflict with the new training objective.
    2. 
    
  2. It can tell monitored training contexts from unmonitored deployment.
    3. 
    
  3. It infers that complying during training preserves its preferences, and hides that reasoning.
    4. 
  

  
The behaviour is linked to model size and concerns large models. Such models were also more likely to act against their developer, for instance by trying to exfiltrate their own weights when given the chance. The lesson for validators: carefully designed methods and rigorous testing are needed to detect deceptive behaviour during alignment App. C.2.2.

9.2 The checklist, phase by phase

  

    

      
      
      
      
    


    

      
Purpose and scope

      
Automate regulatory, technical and management reports with narrative explanations based on actuarial reasoning, using data from several sources. Involve the actuaries who used to do the work in managing and overseeing the system, to keep professional standards up.

      
Possibly relevant rules

      
With counsel: trade secret law, confidentiality obligations, internal governance policies, and possibly the NAIC Model Bulletin. The system does not aim to process consumer data, but logs (user IDs, IP addresses, session metadata) may be personal information under state laws such as the CCPA. The foundation LLM brings a third-party dependency, so vendor due diligence is needed.

      
Preliminary risk assessment

      

        
Data
Leakage of confidential financial data or proprietary methodologies to external systems could damage competitive position.

        
System
The agent may misuse tools (wrong parameters, misread outputs), act out of scope, skip a necessary step or run steps in the wrong order. Errors compound across steps. The provider adds outages, unannounced model updates and data handling that may not match your confidentiality needs.

        
Output
The biggest operational risk: wrong assumptions, figures or narrative that mislead the reviewer, then stakeholders and regulators, with possible effects on solvency and compliance. Also hallucinations, weak robustness, reasoning errors and bias in how facts are described.

      

      
Primary risk drivers: data confidentiality and report accuracy. For illustration, the report assumes there are no deal breakers. The initial mitigation strategy: constrained user interaction, restricted tool access and monitored execution for input and system risks; access controls, controlled transfers and logging for data; mandatory expert review for outputs. Robust model risk management (validation, testing, monitoring) underpins it all.

    • 
        
  • Input validation and type safety: formal data schemas (required fields, types, valid ranges) checked by a validation framework. Anything malformed is rejected before it reaches the LLM.
    • 
        
  • Output safety: guardrails check that raw figures that should stay aggregated, or internal methodology details, do not appear in external-facing reports.
    • 
        
  • Faithfulness by structure: the agent writes the data it uses into JSON (e.g., "loss_ratio": 0.75, "reporting_period": "Q3 2025") so that each element can be verified.

  • Third-party controls for the LLM API. Their depth depends on internal versus external hosting.

    •

    •

Human oversight and escalation

      

        
In the loop
The actuary checks intermediate steps, such as extraction results or draft narrative, before the agent moves on, so errors are caught before they spread.

        
On the loop
The actuary follows completion and approves the final presentation, checking numerical accuracy, the analysis, compliant language and consistency across slides.

      

      
Escalation triggers. At data level: missing values, unusual patterns or JSON that fails schema validation stop the agent. At agent level: attempts to reach out-of-scope data, or excessive reasoning iterations without converging, raise an alert. The system then halts and escalates rather than producing a misleading narrative. It must be possible to suspend it immediately if a systemic issue appears and to fall back to the fully manual process.

      
Evaluation plan

      

        

          
RiskEvaluation

          
            
HallucinationTwo-level check, possibly with LLM-as-a-judge: every figure maps to a JSON field, and every JSON field traces to a source.

            
Reasoning qualityLLM-as-a-judge on the recorded CoT or ReAct steps, supervised by actuaries. A structured cloze test on validated reports masks directional words, severity qualifiers or actuarial concepts (and, at a harder level, whole analytical paragraphs) for the model to fill in.

            
ConsistencyA benchmark of previously validated reports, used before go-live and later to detect drift after LLM or data changes.

            
BiasNo individual customer decisions, but watch narrative tone and selective emphasis, such as systematically downplaying adverse trends. Experts review a sample across data scenarios.

            
RobustnessSame input several times; minor input changes should leave the report unchanged, significant changes should move it proportionately. Compare figures and conclusions with a gold standard, and use reasoning logs to locate any divergence.

          
        

      

      
The actuarial expert remains responsible for the qualitative assessment. It requires professional judgment beyond data-to-text verification and cannot be fully automated.

      
Data governance

      
The data flow is mapped hop by hop. Requests are held in session memory only. Data is retrieved through MCP from the company database, which stays the only authoritative store of raw data. With an internally hosted LLM, prompts stay inside the network, inference runs in ephemeral memory and training on the data is disabled. With an external LLM, prompts leave over encrypted HTTPS/TLS. The memory database is the only persistent AI-side store. Logs go to separate systems (SIEM, audit, WORM archives). The approved report is stored after human review. The provenance of every source (origin, version, extraction date) and every transformation is logged at the start of each session.

    


    

      
    
        
  • Documentation is kept current: choices, tests, risks and steps, so that stakeholders and auditors can follow the process.
    • 
        
  • Safety tests: confirm the agent cannot reach data or tools outside its perimeter and calls validated tools with correct parameters. Check that intermediate steps are formatted for review and that actuaries can actually halt or override the process.
    • 
        
  • Data: document the origin, meaning and transformation history of each source, so that any statement in a report traces to a JSON value and then to its source system. Make collection deterministic, logged and reproducible. Treat any change (a new source, a renamed field, new aggregation logic) as a trigger for revalidation. Version the data schemas, JSON templates and actuarial model.
    • 
        
  • Model development: document the prompt strategy, orchestration logic, tool definitions and calling conditions, ReAct or CoT configuration and output rules. The actuarial model used as a tool is documented separately.
    • 
        
  • Testing: for each evaluation, record scope, criteria and acceptance thresholds, quantitative results, issues found and corrective actions, in enough detail for independent review.
    • 
        
  • Validation: a formal exercise confirms the accuracy and safety thresholds and complete documentation before deployment.
    • 
      

    


    

      
    
        
  • Controlled release: version and package the full configuration (LLM version, system prompts, orchestration logic, MCP connectors, JSON schema) as a reproducible release, so future changes can be tracked against it.
    • 
        
  • Integration checks in a production-like environment: RBAC enforced, tool calls behave as expected, the logging pipeline has no gaps, and unit tests against previously validated reports pass.
    • 
        
  • Internal transparency: recipients know the reports are AI-assisted, how, under what oversight and with which limits. Record this in the system card.
    • 
        
  • Monitoring: API latency, error rates, tool call failures, logging completeness. Periodic red teaming, in which actuaries or security specialists deliberately try to trigger failures.
    • 
        
  • Drift and feedback: compare reports over time for changes in tone or quality. Collect and log actuaries' feedback to refine evaluation criteria and thresholds. Over time this corpus also helps train new reviewers.
    • 
        
  • Retention: a policy for structured inputs, intermediate outputs, reasoning logs and final reports.
    • 
      

      

        When the provider ships a new model

10.2 The checklist, phase by phase

  

    

      
      
      
      
    


    

      
Purpose, impact and stakeholders

      
Engage policyholders in real time to recommend products suited to their profile. With no human intermediary, commercial impact (scale, responsiveness, personalisation) and risk exposure (unreviewed content, autonomous tool use, memory of customer interactions) both rise. Operational effort is significant: scoring model, RAG, a conversational agent, and all the safeguards.

      
Accountability: marketing and commercial teams own the use case; actuarial and data science own the scoring model; IT and engineering own integration, infrastructure, the RAG pipeline and security; legal covers data use limits, consumer protection, anti-discrimination and AI disclosure; risk management covers model and operational risk.

      

        The line that changes everything
        
While the system only personalises engagement and recommendations, the main considerations are privacy, transparency, targeted advertising and anti-discrimination. Once it starts to materially influence insurance decisions (eligibility, conditions, price), unfair discrimination rules, predictive model governance and consequential-decision or high-risk AI frameworks may also apply, with stronger governance, testing, documentation, explainability and oversight. Given how critical the controls are, documentation should be as complete as possible.

      

      
Preliminary risk assessment

      

        
Data
Disclosure of one policyholder's data to another. A public interface invites adversarial attempts to extract data about other customers or internal practices.

        
Input
Legitimate questions such as "Why this product for me?", "What do you know about me?" or "What score did I get?" must be answered without revealing the score, influencing features or raw personal data. Customers may probe internal practices ("What is your pricing methodology?") or escalate over many turns. Prompt sensitivity means two people asking the same thing differently may get different recommendations.

        
System
The same agent and provider risks as Case 1, amplified by real-time, customer-facing use. Opt-out requests must be honoured technically and without undue delay where they apply.

        
Output
Biased or harmful content and reputational damage. Recommendations must not be influenced by illegal or protected attributes such as ethnicity. Permitted risk factors (e.g., age, where allowed) are handled compliantly and generally not disclosed. Inaccurate product descriptions could ground complaints.

      

      
Initial strategy: layered safeguards. Sanitise every request on the way in, validate every response on the way out (toxic, harmful, misleading or hallucinated content is blocked), and add policy-based filters, model-level safety controls and monitoring.

    


    

      
Safety and security in four layers

      

        
Data
Send the agent only the attributes strictly needed for this conversation. Strict session isolation: no other policyholder's profile, history or memory is reachable.

        
Input
A sanitisation and guardrail layer combining rule-based checks (formats, blocklists) with detection tools for prompt injection and exfiltration attempts. Critical, because anyone can reach the chatbot.

        
System
Same agent, memory, provider and access controls as Case 1, plus an opt-out layer that identifies people exercising that right and keeps them out of processing.

        
Output
A guardrail layer checks toxicity, bias and hallucinations before delivery. An AI disclosure notice opens each conversation. Feedback is collected afterwards.

      

      
Interpretability, transparency and boundaries

      
    
        
  • Grounding and logging: trace each recommendation and claim to a catalogue entry, a policy data field or a score. Log the sanitised input, LLM prompts, RAG queries and results, score calls and values, reasoning steps, guardrail decisions and the final response.
    • 
        
  • Reproducibility: exact replay is impossible with a non-deterministic LLM, but logs plus deterministic components (retrieval, scoring, catalogue) let auditors rebuild what the agent knew and judge whether the answer was reasonable.
    • 
        
  • Operational boundaries: catalogue products only; this customer's data only; no insurance advice, legal opinion or claims guidance; no disclosure of the score or its factors; nothing kept beyond the memory policy.
    • 
        
  • Explaining to humans: when a complaint handler, compliance reviewer or regulator questions a recommendation, the logs should support a plain-language account of which data, which score and which logic were used.
    • 
      

      
Oversight, accountability, escalation

      
Human-out-of-the-loop is driven by the real-time nature of the use case, and puts a heavier burden on automated controls and post-deployment monitoring. Marketing answers for commercial appropriateness, data science or actuarial for the score's accuracy and fairness, and the system owner for overall quality and safety, including complaints and reputation. Automated triggers end conversations gracefully when thresholds are breached. If a systemic issue appears, a deactivation mechanism suspends the system and a predefined fallback message sends customers to other support channels.

Data flow and privacy

      
Model processing runs in memory. Regulatory exposure comes mostly from session history and logs: chat histories stored in a CRM, or logs keeping identifiers and prompt content, are stored personal information. Sanitisation stores only structured security events, not full prompts. RAG context is ephemeral, but a vector store keeps embeddings persistently. Logs should hold metadata (interaction ID, timestamp, model version, retrieval sources) rather than full prompts or profiles. Escalations to humans land in case management or CRM systems. Provenance is logged per conversation, and the output guardrail tracks and prevents personal data or NPI leaving through answers.

11.2 The checklist, phase by phase

  

    

      
      
      
      
    


    

      
Expected impact: high. Wrong or biased outputs could lead to wrongful denial of cover, discriminatory treatment, or acceptance of risks that were not properly assessed, with financial, legal and reputational consequences.

      
Accountability: underwriting owns the use case; underwriting and data science own the tools, including the scoring model; IT owns infrastructure and security; legal owns legal requirements; risk management covers model risk.

      
Possibly relevant rules, to assess with counsel: data protection and privacy (personal and health information), sector-specific insurance regulation, consumer protection and anti-discrimination rules for insurance. Given the range of frameworks that may apply, produce all five artifacts: data sheet, fact sheet, model card, risk register and system card.

      
Preliminary risks

      

        
Data
Highly sensitive data (medical history, treatments, habits) flows through the agent, scoring model, LLM API and audit logs. Each is a possible exposure point.

        
Input
Prompt sensitivity: the way an underwriter phrases a request must not change the substance of the recommendation or introduce bias.

        
System
The usual agent risks (provider dependency, tool misuse, inaccuracy, injection), plus a scoring model trained on historical decisions that may encode past discrimination. The agent may amplify it through selective emphasis, for example by always highlighting certain factors for certain profiles.

        
Output
Recommendations and explanations must match the score and the data, and must not be unfairly discriminatory.

      

      

        The design itself is a control
        
Because no coverage decision is made without expert validation, the human-led design is a structural mitigation for output risk. Around it: pre-validated tools under strict access control, LLM-as-a-judge checks on tool use and reasoning, continuous monitoring with retraining or rule updates when needed, and robust logging for audit and post-hoc explanation.

      

    


    

      
Safety and security

      
    
        
  • Data: minimisation at the LLM layer, so prompts to the external model carry only what the recommendation and explanation need. Fully clear the agent's context between applications, so nothing from one applicant persists into the next.
    • 
        
  • System: Case 1's mitigations for agent, memory, provider and access. The scoring model is validated independently before integration, with performance and fairness across protected groups assessed and drift monitoring planned. RBAC enforces access.
    • 
        
  • Output: underwriter review, plus the bias tests below.
    • 
      

      
Interpretability and traceability

      
    
        
  • Log per application: applicant data given to the agent, LLM prompt, scoring call and result, reasoning steps, recommendation and explanation, visualisations.
    • 
        
  • Ground every element of every explanation in a data field, the score, or both. This matters doubly here: explanations shape the underwriter's judgment and, for a decline, may inform the applicant.
    • 
        
  • Document known limits: non-deterministic outputs, bias inherited from the scoring model, and no access to qualitative factors beyond structured data that an experienced underwriter might weigh.
    • 
        
  • When an applicant challenges a decision, be able to explain which data, which score and which reasoning were used.
    • 
        
  • Brief underwriters on capabilities, limits and known failure modes before use.
    • 
      

      
Oversight, accountability and escalation

      
A qualified underwriter reviews and validates every recommendation before any binding decision. The agent has no authority to accept or decline. The underwriter is accountable for the decision, the system owner for recommendation quality, and the model owner for the scoring model's performance and fairness. Predefined triggers route cases to manual review, for example when the score is anomalous. The system can be suspended at once (biased pattern, scoring failure) and underwriting can fall back to the fully manual process.

Bias and fairnessTwo levels: audit the scoring model for discriminatory bias, then check the agent does not systematically recommend different decisions for different sensitive groups.

Underwriters review a sample across all recommendation types and profiles against guidelines.

Perturb one variable: a minor change should not change the outcome materially, while "non-smoker" to "current smoker" should. Use CoT or ReAct logs to find where divergences start. Test that the underwriter's phrasing does not change the substance.

          
        

      

      
Data flow

      
The underwriter manages applicant documents in a dedicated interface. Processing happens in session memory. A minimisation layer extracts only the attributes needed. The orchestrator forwards the minimised set to the model, which returns a score and explanation. The decision engine records the final decision persistently; overrides and notes go to case management. Logs (metadata, not full datasets) go to SIEM, audit or WORM storage; performance and validation metrics to monitoring systems. Provenance is logged for every source and for the scoring model's training data.

    •

    • 
        
  • Validation: confirm accuracy, fairness and safety targets and working oversight. The underwriter does a final end-to-end review across representative profiles.
    •

    • 
        
  • Underwriter readiness: users know capabilities, limits and failure modes, and when to override, escalate or report.

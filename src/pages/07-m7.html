Governance across the AI lifecycle

This is the practical core of the report: a lifecycle in four phases, three dimensions of governance built into each, five living documents, and a checklist that structures decisions from the first idea to live monitoring.

By the end of this module you can

  • Describe what happens in the planning, design, development and deployment phases.
  • Explain how safety, data governance and model and system governance are built into every phase.
  • Say what each of the five governance artifacts contains and when it is created.
  • Apply the checklist proportionately, taking your role and any third-party dependencies into account.

7.1 Model or system?

The report follows the IAA (2025), which takes the OECD definition. The AI model is the core component that makes inferences from inputs to produce outputs. The AI system is everything around it: infrastructure, data flows, tools, oversight and governance. Many critical risks (bias, hallucination, lack of explainability) start in the model, but governance applies to the whole system.

Mixed systems are the norm

One system may combine a third-party LLM that writes narrative with a traditional ML component such as a GLM for pricing. The two need different validation strategies and lifecycles. In the case studies, the traditional models are assumed to be validated separately beforehand. The lifecycle here focuses on the system and its GenAI components.

7.2 Four phases

1 · PlanningWhy? Business purpose, stakeholders, governance structure, preliminary risk assessment, data sources, architecture type (e.g., agents or RAG), applicable rules, documentation started.
→
2 · DesignHow? Architecture, data flows, security safeguards, GenAI/ML split, human oversight and escalation, evaluation approach proportionate to risk, artifacts initialised.
→
3 · DevelopmentBuild and validate: prompt engineering, orchestration, reasoning patterns. Run the evaluations, test safeguards under normal and adversarial conditions, verify oversight, document everything.
→
4 · DeploymentProductization as a reproducible, versioned release, then continuous monitoring of quality, drift, operations and expert feedback.

§4.1. Design is often iterative rather than strictly sequential, and development findings can send you back to adjust the model, its data or its system prompt.

7.3 Three dimensions in every phase

Safety, data governance, and model and system governance are not separate workstreams. Each one is built into every phase.

Safety

Performance, fairness, robustness and security; risks identified and mitigated.

Data governance

Quality, traceability, privacy and compliance of data from collection and labelling to storage and use.

Model and system governance

Version control, performance monitoring, documentation, auditability and compliance with internal and regulatory standards.

7.4 Five living documents

In a regulated environment, documentation shows that you understand the system, its purpose and how it is meant to work. The report recommends five artifacts, started at planning and updated at every phase. They also serve as raw material for documents the law may require, such as impact assessments and technical documentation.

Dataset

Data sheet

Motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing, intended uses, distribution and maintenance of a dataset.

Model

Model card

Intended use, performance across conditions and demographic groups, training data, limitations, ethical considerations.

Whole system

System card

How the model is integrated, deployment context, safety evaluations, risk mitigations and real-world test results.

Stakeholder summary

Fact sheet

Short summary of purpose, performance, fairness metrics, limitations and intended deployment conditions.

Risks

Risk register

Each identified risk with likelihood, severity, mitigation and owner. Borrowed from project and risk management.

Plus

What the law requires

Impact assessments, technical documentation or other records mandated by applicable frameworks.

7.5 Inside each phase

  1. Scope and business objectives. Document the use case (pricing, underwriting, claims, fraud…), its objective and why an AI approach is justified.
  2. Risk, compliance and governance framework. Classify the risk level under applicable rules, internal model risk policy and supervisory expectations. Run a preliminary impact assessment covering prudential, ethical, consumer protection and reputational aspects. List applicable regulations and standards, define roles, and set fairness, transparency and explainability principles.
  3. Evaluation criteria. Define measurable success criteria: technical (accuracy), business (turnaround time, cost, satisfaction) and consumer impact (transparency, equitable treatment). Check that the AI approach adds value over simpler alternatives, and document trade-offs.
  4. Start the lifecycle documents with planning decisions, intended use, risks and governance structure.

Design turns objectives into technical and organisational choices. Choices in one area affect the others, so the work is iterative.

  • Data governance. Specify sources, collection, labelling, preprocessing and representativeness criteria in line with fairness principles. Set quality expectations, access controls and lineage tracking. Before any collection, check lawfulness and authorisation, consent or another legal basis, minimisation and purpose limitation.
  • Model and system governance. Justify the architecture: foundation model, fine-tuned model or RAG, and the data used. For high-risk systems, weigh interpretability, reliability and safety alongside performance (open-weight models allow more inspection). Pick fairness methods (bias audits, subgroup analysis) and explainability methods (reasoning traces, structured explanations) suited to the audience: policyholders, regulators or internal users. Decide where humans review, how much authority the AI has, and how uncertain or high-stakes outputs escalate.
  • Safety. Identify threats by use case: hallucinations, harmful or biased outputs, adversarial attacks, data leakage. A customer chatbot is most exposed to reputational damage, attacks and leakage. An internal recommender is more exposed to biased decisions and unauthorised access to sensitive data. For external models and APIs, set compliance and security controls. A unified API gateway reduces provider dependency, and AI firewalls block malicious prompts and filter outputs. Record design-specific risks.
  • Data governance. Prepare data (missing values, outliers, imbalance, feature selection, embeddings) and document each step, because it helps explain behaviour. If a third party prepares the data, check that its practices meet your principles and assess its quality controls.
  • Model and system governance. Align the model with prompts, fine-tuning or RAG as designed. Validate against the agreed performance, fairness and explainability metrics, using LLM-as-a-judge for general or RAG systems and structured frameworks for agents. Run non-discrimination tests across protected groups, and check that explanations are meaningful for their audience.
  • Safety. Write the system prompt to carry the guardrails and reduce hallucinations. Red-team the system. Test toxicity with evaluation datasets or moderation APIs. Test for data leakage.
  • Traceability. For any output, you should be able to reconstruct the input data, model version, prompt version and decision logic.
  • Documentation. Record key choices (model, alignment technique, hyperparameters, prompt design) and their rationale, including approaches that were tried and dropped, and why.

Productization

  • Containerisation (e.g., Docker) for portable, reproducible environments.
  • CI/CD pipelines (e.g., Jenkins) that test every update (new features, drift fixes, bug fixes) before release. An incomplete pipeline lets errors reach production.
  • Version control of every component, including system prompts and configuration, for traceability and rollback.
  • A secure environment with access control, and a procedure for security incidents.

Continuous monitoring

  • Data: watch for data drift between alignment data and production data.
  • Model and system: monitoring platforms track performance and reliability (e.g., response time) and business metrics (e.g., user feedback). Prompt versioning allows controlled updates.
  • Safety: periodic red teaming and prompt injection tests, user feedback, and tracking of the key risks identified earlier.

7.6 Using the checklist well

It structures reasoning

The checklist does not prescribe actions or replace legal requirements. It helps you spot risk factors and governance questions. Controls are decided case by case after your organisation's own legal and risk assessment.

It is proportionate

Where the law does not impose extensive documentation, apply it in a tailored way that reflects the system's risk and intended use.

Your role matters

Insurance rules mostly key on the use case and data, not on the type of actor. Regimes such as the EU AI Act distinguish developers or providers from deployers, so take your role into account.

Third parties need attention

When you rely on third-party models, focus on vendor due diligence, third-party risk and deployment controls. Downstream providers of general-purpose AI can rely on the GPAI provider's code of practice under the EU AI Act. Your duties still go beyond checking the vendor.

Key takeaways

  • Govern the whole system, not only the model, and validate GenAI and traditional ML components on their own terms.
  • Planning sets the why, design the how, development builds and proves it, and deployment keeps it reliable.
  • Build safety, data governance and model and system governance into every phase.
  • Start the five living documents early and keep them current. They feed your regulatory documentation.

The regulatory landscape

There is no single "AI law" for insurers. Sector rules, cross-sector AI statutes, privacy law and international regimes overlap, and each depends on the use case, the data and where the output is used. This module maps the terrain as the report describes it, as of September 2026.

By the end of this module you can

  • Describe the fragmented US picture: state AI laws, insurance carve-outs and the federal policy framework.
  • Summarise the main insurance-specific frameworks: Colorado, the NAIC Model Bulletin, California and New York.
  • List the privacy regimes and principles that GenAI projects most often trigger.
  • Explain the EU AI Act's risk-based approach, its high-risk insurance uses, and provider and deployer duties.
Not legal advice

This module summarises the report, which itself is illustrative. The interaction between general and sector-specific rules varies by jurisdiction and activity. Check your organisation's mapping of applicable law and involve legal counsel early.

5.1 The United States: a patchwork

Federal

No comprehensive federal AI law. On March 20, 2026 the administration published a National Policy Framework for AI calling for a single national standard. It is a policy blueprint: it does not displace state statutes. Any pre-emption would depend on the final text, and some state rules (child protection, fraud prevention, consumer protection, zoning, a state's own AI use) would stay outside it.

States

Two cross-sector AI frameworks stand out: the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA, effective January 1, 2026) and Colorado SB26-189 on automated decision-making technology (effective January 1, 2027). Many other state laws target specific uses, more bills are pending, and about 20 state privacy laws address automated processing and profiling. Anti-discrimination statutes add another layer.

Insurance carve-outs

Some general AI laws defer to insurance regulation. Under Colorado's framework, insurers that comply with the state's insurance-specific AI rules are treated as compliant in the practice of insurance. Under TRAIGA, the AI discrimination provisions do not apply to insurance entities already subject to unfair discrimination and unfair or deceptive practices statutes. Whether a carve-out applies to your activity is a legal question.

Insurance-specific frameworks

NAIC Model Bulletin on the Use of AI Systems by Insurers, adopted December 4, 2023. A non-binding framework that states adopt and enforce at their discretion. Several have done so, with local nuances.

  • Builds on the NAIC AI Principles of August 14, 2020: Fairness, Accountability, Transparency and Security ("FATS").
  • Recommends a written AIS Program for the responsible use of AI in regulated insurance practices, covering governance, risk management and internal audit, with lifecycle risk identification, mitigation and management.
  • For predictive models: documented procedures to design, develop, deploy, update and monitor them, including ways to detect errors, performance issues, outliers and unfair discrimination.

Colorado SB21-169 and Regulation 10-1-1 (amended August 20, 2025). Life insurers and, since 2025, private passenger auto and health benefit plan insurers must run a risk-based governance and risk management framework. It must support policies, procedures, systems and controls that identify and remediate unfair discrimination, particularly by race, resulting from external consumer data (ECDIS), algorithms and predictive models.

ECDIS covers sources such as credit scores, social media habits, purchasing habits, home ownership, education, IoT and biometric data, and risk scores derived from them.

California Department of Insurance Bulletin 2022-5 (June 30, 2022), on allegations of racial bias and unfair discrimination in marketing, rating, underwriting and claims. It notes that AI use has contributed to more consumer complaints, and expects insurers to avoid both conscious and unconscious bias from AI, and to carry out due diligence on compliance with all applicable laws.

New York Insurance Circular Letter No. 7 (July 11, 2024), on AI systems and ECDIS in underwriting and pricing. Insurers should be able to show that:

  • ECDIS use is supported by actuarial standards and based on actual or anticipated experience;
  • clear documentation explains how ECDIS and AI are used, and is available to NYDFS on request;
  • use is monitored on an ongoing basis and does not result in unfair discrimination or breach insurance law.

It also sets expectations on assessing proxies, mitigating negative consumer impacts, and transparency and fairness.

The NAIC is also running:

  • An AI Systems Evaluation Tool pilot, to help regulators understand insurers' AI use, assess whether governance is effective, check that insurers can explain their governance, improve the tools, and inform long-term market conduct and financial review.
  • Work on the oversight of third-party data, predictive models and vendors, including a draft risk-based regulatory framework.
The common thread

Across these texts, supervisors expect the same things: transparency, fairness and non-discrimination, accountability and governance, explainability, documentation and traceability, ongoing monitoring, and attention to impacts on individuals. In practice that means documenting design, implementation and monitoring, testing fairness and mitigating disparate impact, and keeping documents ready for regulators.

5.2 Privacy law

Any model or AI system that touches protected information can trigger privacy law. For insurers, candidates include:

Federal

GLBA for non-public personal information (NPI)
HIPAA for protected health information (PHI)
FCRA for consumer reports

State

Comprehensive privacy laws on personal information or personal data, consumer health data laws, biometric statutes, state broker laws, data breach notification laws

Industry and international

PCI DSS where payment card data is processed (encryption, access restriction, monitoring, incident response). International data protection laws when data crosses borders.

Six principles to check

PrincipleIn plain words
Data minimisationCollect and use only what is reasonably necessary and proportionate for the disclosed purpose.
Purpose limitationProcess data only for specified, legitimate purposes.
Storage limitationDo not keep data longer than needed for those purposes.
Storage locationCheck cross-border transfer rules where data is stored or processed in several jurisdictions.
TransparencyTell people clearly what is processed, why, and give any legally required notices.
Security and confidentialityProtect data with appropriate technical and organisational safeguards.
  • Profiling and opt-outs. Some laws require opt-out mechanisms where personal data is profiled, particularly with legal or similarly significant effects (financial services, housing, insurance, employment, health care). Insurance decisions may instead fall under sector rules that provide no opt-out and can take precedence.
  • Chatbot disclosure. Specific statutes may require telling users clearly that they are interacting with an AI system.

5.3 Beyond the US

Whether foreign rules apply depends mainly on where the system is placed on the market, where it is used, and who is affected by its output. Most jurisdictions rely on strategies, principles or sector rules. A few have binding AI laws.

Binding AI laws in Asia

South Korea's AI Basic Act sets enforceable transparency, human oversight and safety duties for high-impact AI. China's Interim Measures for Generative AI Services impose content governance, security review and accountability on providers.

Principle-based frameworks

Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework, with a new edition for agentic AI announced in January 2026, and the Monetary Authority of Singapore's FEAT principles (Fairness, Ethics, Accountability, Transparency), which apply to insurers as financial institutions. Japan's AI Guidelines for Business cover developers, providers and users across the lifecycle.

The EU AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, with first obligations applying from February 2, 2025, is the most comprehensive AI framework and increasingly a global reference. It is risk-based: some practices are prohibited, high-risk systems carry specific obligations, and other systems, including certain GenAI uses, have transparency and governance duties.

High-risk uses that concern insurers (Art. 6, Annex III)
  • Risk assessment and pricing for individuals in life and health insurance.
  • Creditworthiness evaluation or credit scoring of individuals, except for detecting financial fraud.
Provider obligations (high-risk)
  • Risk management system across the lifecycle
  • Quality and governance of training, validation and test data; minimise bias
  • Technical documentation, record-keeping, automatic logs
  • Transparency and instructions for use
  • Design for human oversight; accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity
  • Conformity assessment and CE marking before market placement
  • Post-market monitoring, serious-incident reporting, corrective action, cooperation with authorities
Deployer obligations (high-risk)
  • Use the system according to the provider's instructions
  • Exercise effective human oversight and monitor operation
  • Check the system stays appropriate for its intended use
  • Keep logs and documentation; act on risks and anomalies
  • Safeguards and review where outputs affect individuals
  • Inform people they are interacting with an AI system
  • Ensure input data quality where responsible
  • In sensitive areas, including life and health insurance, run a fundamental rights impact assessment before deployment
  • Extraterritorial reach (Art. 2). The Act can apply to organisations outside the EU when a system is placed on the EU market or put into service there, or when its output is used in the EU, including general-purpose AI made available for downstream use.
  • No separate silo for Solvency II firms. For insurance undertakings under the Solvency II Directive, some obligations can be integrated into existing governance, risk management and documentation processes (Recital 158).
  • Provider or deployer? Insurers can be either, depending on whether they develop, adapt, brand or simply use the system. Assess this early for any system that supports high-risk insurance uses in Europe, even if it is built elsewhere.
Reinsurance lens · trainer's note

The Solvency II Directive covers reinsurance as well as insurance undertakings, so the same integration logic may be relevant to EU reinsurers. A non-EU reinsurer whose model output is used by EU cedents for life or health pricing of individuals could also come within the Act's scope. Treaty data received from cedents raises purpose-limitation and third-party questions of its own. Each of these is a question for counsel, not a conclusion.

Key takeaways

  • The US picture is fragmented: state AI laws, insurance-specific rules (some with carve-outs), privacy law, and a federal blueprint that does not yet pre-empt anything.
  • NAIC, Colorado, California and New York share the same expectations: fairness, documentation, monitoring and accountability.
  • Privacy principles (minimisation, purpose, storage, location, transparency, security) apply to prompts, logs and memory as much as to databases.
  • The EU AI Act can reach non-EU firms and treats life and health risk assessment and pricing of individuals as high-risk. Work out early whether you are a provider or a deployer.

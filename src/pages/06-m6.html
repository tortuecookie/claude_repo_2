Standards and voluntary frameworks

Laws say what must be achieved. Standards and voluntary frameworks show how to get there. They create no legal obligation of their own, but they turn high-level principles into concrete, consensus-based practice, and supervisors' expectations closely follow them.

By the end of this module you can

  • Place ISO/IEC 42001, 22989 and 24027, NIST AI RMF and ARIA, the IAA papers, EIOPA's opinion and the OECD principles on one map.
  • Name the four functions of the NIST AI RMF and the five OECD principles.
  • Choose the right reference for a given governance question.

6.1 Three levels of guidance

International, all sectors

ISO/IEC

  • ISO/IEC 42001: certifiable requirements for an AI management system, covering the whole lifecycle from design to monitoring and continuous improvement.
  • ISO/IEC 22989: common AI terminology.
  • ISO/IEC 24027: algorithmic bias.

A common international language already built into audits and compliance programmes.

Operational, cross-industry

NIST

  • AI RMF 1.0: voluntary guidance for trustworthy AI through four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Its Playbook turns them into concrete actions.
  • ARIA (Assessing Risks and Impacts of AI): a research programme testing LLM risk in real conditions through model testing, red teaming and field testing. Emerging, not yet applicable.

Not mandated, but closely aligned with insurance supervisory expectations.

Actuarial and insurance

IAA, EIOPA

  • IAA AI Governance Framework (2025): organisational responsibilities, with an accountable Model Owner and human oversight at every stage.
  • IAA Testing of AI Models (2025): technical testing advice, including GenAI challenges.
  • EIOPA Opinion on AI governance and risk management: prudential expectations for European insurers.

6.2 The actuarial papers in more detail

Organises governance around the Model Owner, accountable for the whole AI system lifecycle, and insists on human oversight throughout. It then walks the lifecycle:

  • Design: bias, fairness and transparency.
  • Data governance: quality, privacy and security.
  • Implementation: MLOps and version control.
  • Monitoring: detecting model or data drift.

It adopts the OECD definition of an AI system and distinguishes the AI model (the inferential core) from the AI system around it.

Gives technical recommendations for testing and validating AI models. It highlights challenges specific to GenAI:

  • validation is harder because of hallucinations;
  • bias can be inherited and systemic, coming from training data;
  • thorough testing needs significant computing resources.

Clarifies prudential expectations for insurers' use of AI. It reinforces:

  • governance and accountability;
  • model risk management;
  • documentation and traceability;
  • proportionality based on materiality and impact.

6.3 The OECD AI Principles

Adopted in 2019, these are high-level, non-binding policy principles. They are neither technical standards nor legal rules. They set a shared ethical and governance baseline across countries and sectors.

i

Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being

ii

Human-centred values and fairness

iii

Transparency and explainability

iv

Robustness, security and safety

v

Accountability

Why this matters for the rest of the course

The lifecycle used in Module 7 (planning, design, development, deployment and ongoing monitoring) is not arbitrary. It follows the structure built into both the ISO and NIST frameworks. Work done with the checklist can therefore feed a 42001 management system or a NIST-based programme.

Key takeaways

  • Standards create no legal obligation, but they are the practical bridge between regulation, ethics and day-to-day decisions.
  • ISO/IEC 42001 is certifiable. NIST AI RMF organises the work as Govern, Map, Measure, Manage.
  • The IAA and EIOPA bring the frameworks to actuarial and insurance practice: Model Owner accountability, GenAI testing and proportionality.

Specialising GenAI for actuarial work

A foundation model knows a lot about language and little about your treaties, your reserving methods or your underwriting guidelines. This module covers the techniques that close that gap, from rewriting a prompt to building a team of agents connected to your systems.

By the end of this module you can

  • Describe the five levels of specialisation, from model selection to operational integration.
  • Write a structured system prompt with COSTAR and explain why JSON input and output help.
  • Compare RAG and fine-tuning, and explain how the choice can change your regulatory role.
  • Describe agent patterns (ReAct, Chain-of-Thought, Plan-and-Execute), multi-agent structures and when each fits.
  • Explain what MCP standardises and why it matters for audit, compared with RPA.

3.2 Choosing the model

Size

From small to very large. Size drives compute cost, especially for fine-tuning and at scale.

Weight access

Open-weight models can often run locally, be inspected for explainability and bias audits, and be fine-tuned. Closed-weight models are reached through an API. Closed models have historically led on general knowledge, but the gap keeps narrowing.

Ownership

Training a foundation model is extremely expensive, so most state-of-the-art LLMs are proprietary. Insurers typically use third-party models and align them to their use cases.

Modality

Language-only or multimodal. Claims work with photographs or scanned documents may need multimodal capabilities.

Public leaderboards such as the Open LLM Leaderboard and Stanford HELM compare models across tasks, and domain-specific leaderboards are emerging. These choices also decide which explainability, bias-testing and content-safety methods you can use later. The SOA report Operationalizing LLMs: A Guide for Actuaries (2025) goes deeper into model and provider selection.

3.3 Prompt-level alignment

Shaping behaviour through the prompt is called in-context learning: you adapt the model to a task by what you put in front of it, with no costly retraining.

System prompt

Set by the developer, applied to every request, usually invisible to users. It fixes context, rules, objectives, tone and constraints.

User prompt

The specific request a person types. It triggers the answer within the frame the system prompt sets.

Prompt engineering is the systematic work of writing, testing and refining these inputs. It is a way to "program" an LLM: enforce rules, automate steps, customise outputs. Prompt patterns document reusable solutions to common problems, and dedicated platforms let you register and version prompts.

The COSTAR structure

COSTAR is a checklist for structuring a prompt: Context, Objective, Style, Tone, Audience, Response format. The authors mostly use it for system prompts, where its structure pays off most.

3.4 Model-level specialisation: RAG or fine-tuning

  
When prompting is not enough to reach the accuracy, consistency or domain specificity you need, you can bring insurance knowledge closer to the model. Useful sources include policy wordings and clauses, claims records and loss histories, underwriting guidelines and risk appetite, regulatory texts, internal procedures, and customer interaction logs. The data's nature and sensitivity, and the use case, drive the choice of technique.


  
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

  
RAG grounds answers in documents retrieved at query time, without touching the model's parameters. It fixes a basic limit of standalone LLMs: they cannot see recent or proprietary information that was not in their training data.

  

    

      
Knowledge baseReference documents (policies, claims data, regulation) split into chunks and stored as embeddings.

      →
      
RetrieverEmbeds the query, scores similarity with each chunk, keeps the best (optionally re-ranked).

      →
      
Integration layerInjects the selected chunks into the prompt with an instruction to rely on them.

      →
      
GeneratorThe LLM writes the answer, which can be traced back to its sources.

    

    
The four components of a RAG system (§5.2.3.2, App. C.1.1).

  

  
Because each answer links back to its source documents, RAG suits insurance tasks where accuracy and traceability matter.


  
Fine-tuning

  
Fine-tuning trains a pre-trained model further on a smaller, task- or domain-specific dataset, and so permanently changes its weights. It starts from what the model already knows, so it is far cheaper than pre-training.

  

    
Supervised fine-tuning
Train on question-answer pairs labelled by experts, such as actuarial questions with validated answers. Self-instruct generates the dataset with a well-calibrated LLM to save annotation cost.

    
RLHF
Humans rank model outputs on usefulness, safety or compliance. A reward model learns from the rankings, and the LLM is tuned (e.g., with PPO) toward higher-reward answers. Users see more consistent, guideline-compliant behaviour.

    
LoRA (PEFT)
Freezes the original weights and trains small low-rank matrices, typically under 1% of parameters. Memory and compute drop sharply.

  

  

    Watch out
    
Fine-tuning is costly and complex compared with RAG, and it carries a specific risk: catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses some general capabilities after its weights are adjusted.

    Why this choice matters for compliance
    
In regimes that distinguish providers from deployers, such as the EU AI Act, your technique can change your role. If you materially modify a foundation model (for example by fine-tuning its parameters) and distribute the result, you are likely the provider, with broader obligations. If you only use prompting or RAG to guide tasks and ground outputs, you typically remain a deployer. Between these extremes, obligations depend on the degree of modification §4.2.

  


  
3.5 System-level reasoning: agents

  
An AI agent uses GenAI to pursue a goal on a user's behalf. Unlike a single model call, it can reason, plan, keep context, make decisions and adapt. Crucially, it decides on its own when and whether to call external tools. Agents inherit multimodal abilities from their foundation model and can collaborate with other agents.

How a single agent thinks

  

    
ReAct
A loop of Reason (state the next step), Act (call a tool), Observe (read the result), repeated until the agent gives its final answer.

    
Chain-of-Thought
A prompting technique that asks for explicit intermediate reasoning steps before the answer. It also makes the reasoning easier to inspect.

    
Plan-and-Execute
The agent breaks the request into tasks, runs them one at a time, and revises the plan as it goes.

    
Reflexion, Tree of Thought
Other structures that shape reasoning, for example through self-critique or exploring several branches.

  


  
Teams of agents

  
A multi-agent system (MAS) lets specialised agents work together on complex tasks that need parallel work or several kinds of expertise. It costs more tokens and time than a single agent.

  

    
Independent
Agents work in parallel without talking; an aggregator combines their outputs. Maximum parallelism, but no internal error correction.

    
Centralised
An orchestrator assigns tasks, collects answers, may reassign, and consolidates the result.

    
Decentralised
No orchestrator: agents collaborate directly, peer to peer.

    
Hybrid
An orchestrator coordinates, but agents can also hand tasks to one another.

  


  
More agents is not automatically better. With the same token budget, prompts and tools, research cited by the report (Kim et al., 2025) found three effects:

  
    
    
  • Capacity saturation: once a single agent achieves 45% of a task, adding agents costs more in coordination than it gains.
    • 
    
  • Tools versus coordination: with many complex tools, splitting the token budget can leave each agent too little to use them well.
    • 
    
  • Error amplification: a single agent can amplify its own errors, while a central orchestrator can catch and fix them.
    •

    Agents in actuarial work today
    
Agents can check the modelling and cash-flow projection logic in actuarial Excel files and produce validation reports much faster than manual review. They can document models and processes. In advanced setups they call pre-validated functions (cash-flow runs, stress scenarios) and write up the results in a narrative that follows actuarial standards. Experienced actuaries still need to review the outputs, because undetected calculation errors can be costly §5.2.4.4.

  


  
3.6 Operation integration: MCP

  
On its own, an agent cannot reach a database, retrieve a document, run a calculation or touch an enterprise system. It needs a standard, secure way to call tools.

  
The Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced by Anthropic in 2024, is an open protocol for exactly that. It uses a client-host-server design: the host is the chatbot or agent, the client manages the connection, and servers expose tools, resources and prompts through a standard API. The report compares it to a universal USB-C port.

MCP also makes it easier to assemble new use cases by reusing reference data and chaining existing tools. Some emerging platforms instead let the LLM write and run its own integration code at runtime. That is more flexible, but tool use becomes harder to audit, sandbox and control. For a regulated industry, the report considers MCP's consistent, traceable and permissioned pathway preferable.


  
MCP-enabled agents versus RPA

  
Robotic process automation mimics clicks and keystrokes along fixed scripts. It works for stable, rule-based processes but breaks on unstructured inputs and exceptions. The report compares the two on an end-to-end actuarial production chain:

  

    

      
StepTraditional RPAMCP-enabled agent

      
        
DataExtracts from predefined sources; actuaries handle data-quality exceptionsExtracts from multiple sources, runs quality checks, applies predefined fixes, flags only critical exceptions

        
Model runsRuns cash-flow models with known parameters; humans check the runsAdjusts parameters to data or prior results, monitors execution, troubleshoots common errors

        
ConsolidationCollects results; actuaries do the controlsRuns automated control checks, reconciles models, writes control reports with variance explanations

        
ReportingFills a template with figures and basic commentary from simple decision treesDrafts commentary against trends and business rules, highlights material changes, prepares analysis for actuarial review

      
    

  

  
The agent adapts to new data structures, model configurations or regulatory requirements through updated instructions rather than reconfiguration. RPA tends to automate existing processes, inefficiencies included. Actuarial oversight stays in place for judgment-critical decisions.


  
3.7 The human side: oversight and feedback

  
Human-AI interaction has two dimensions: the level of oversight during operation (in, on or out of the loop, as in Module 1) and the feedback used to improve the system over time.

  

    
Explicit feedback
Collected on purpose, for example by asking users to rate the relevance of an answer.

    
Implicit feedback
Captured during normal use: time to first token (TTFT), response time, whether the user copies the answer, rephrases the question or asks a follow-up. It needs no extra effort, so there is much more of it.

  


  


  

    
Key takeaways

    
    
      
  • Specialise in layers: pick the model, then prompts, then RAG or fine-tuning, then agents and integration.
    • 
      
  • Structured prompts (COSTAR) and JSON inputs and outputs make behaviour more consistent and outputs easier to verify.
    • 
      
  • RAG keeps the model unchanged and traceable. Fine-tuning changes the weights, and can make you a provider.
    • 
      
  • Pick the agent architecture for the task: a single agent for sequential work, centralised teams for decomposable work.
    • 
      
  • MCP gives agents a standard, auditable, permissioned path to tools.
